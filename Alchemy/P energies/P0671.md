Viktor Frankl suggests that we find meaning in life through three primary avenues:

1. **Creating or doing something meaningful** — through work, art, service, or contribution.
    
2. **Experiencing something or someone deeply** — such as love, beauty, nature, or a relationship.
    
3. **Adopting a meaningful attitude toward unavoidable suffering** — by how we choose to face pain, loss, or limitation with dignity.
    

  

His core idea is: **meaning is not something we invent, but something we discover** in the world and in our response to it.

This idea rests on his belief in an **objective dimension of meaning** that exists outside the self. In contrast to purely subjective or hedonistic approaches, he suggests that life **asks something of us**, rather than us demanding something from life.

  

He calls this the **“will to meaning”**, which is deeper than the will to pleasure (Freud) or the will to power (Adler). We uncover meaning by listening to what our circumstances demand from us—like a **dialogue between the soul and life itself**.

  

This turns life into a kind of **existential responsibility**, where we are each uniquely called to respond to our situation with authenticity.
