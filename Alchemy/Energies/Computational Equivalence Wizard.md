**The Principal of Computational Equivalence**  
The following quote is by Dr. Steven Wolfram   
 Creator of Mathematica   
 Author of 'A New Kind Of Science' 

He wrote in the book ‘A New Kind of Science’: 

“Three centuries ago science was transformed by the dramatic new idea that rules based on  mathematical equations could be used to describe the natural world. My purpose in this book is  to initiate another such transformation, and to introduce a new kind of science that is based on  much more general types of rules that can be embodied in simple programs.” “And on the basis of many discoveries I have been led to a still more sweeping conclusion,  summarized in what I call the Principle of Computational Equivalence: that whenever one sees  behavior that is not obviously simple-in essentially any system- it can be thought of as  corresponding to a computation of equivalent sophistication. And this one very basic principle  has a quite unprecedented array of implications for science and scientific thinking.” 

He came across his first discovery by accident in 1981: 

He experimented on a computer by giving it a simple set of instructions for a graphic and seeing  what the results were. He named the process Cellular Automata, a simple set of instructions for  the placement of either a black or white cell on a grid based on the cells above it. On a scientific  level, the patterns created were startling. After further experimentation, he came to several  conclusions, the main one being that it was possible to obtain a system of great complexity from  a very simple set of rules.  

This in itself was interesting, but it led him further. His discoveries have an unprecedented field  of relevance, he foresees their implications in the fields of; mathematics, physics, biology, social  sciences, computer sciences, philosophy, Technology and even art.  

The principle of computational equivalence’s basis is that “**all processes, whether they are  produced by human effort or occur spontaneously in nature, can be viewed as  computations**”. This indicates the growth of a plant to the phenomenon of apparently 'random'  weather to the process of natural selection can all be thought of as computations similar to those  in the basic system of a computer. 

The principle of computational equivalence also contradicts the traditional western thought about  humans and their place in nature. Dr Wolfram wrote in his book A New Kind Of Science: "It  would be most satisfying if science were to prove that we as humans are in some fundamental  way special, and above everything else in the universe. But if one looks at the history of science  many of its greatest advances have come precisely from identifying ways in which we are not  special." 

He goes on to say:    
"Four centuries ago we learned for example that our planet does not lie at a special position in  the universe. A century and a half ago we learned that there was nothing very special about the   
origin of our species. And over the past century we have learned that there is nothing special  about our various physical, chemical and other constituents. 

Yet in western thought there is still a strong belief that there must be something fundamentally  special about us. And nowadays the most common assumption is that it must have to do with the  level of intelligence or complexity that we exhibit. But building on what I have discovered in this  book, the principle of computational equivalence now makes the fairly dramatic statement that  even in these ways there is nothing fundamentally special about us." 

Another revelation the principle of computational equivalence indicates is that "observers tend to  be computationally equivalent to the systems they observe." In a sense, this means that the  observer and the observed are not separate and therefore can affect one another. 

On the scientific level, these discoveries made by Dr. Wolfram appear to be one of the greatest  intellectual revolutions yet. It seems likely they will be ranked with the discovery of gravity,  atoms and the theory of relativity.
