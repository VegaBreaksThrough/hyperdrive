There is a great neglect of ‘being’ and a great focus (or interest) in ‘becoming’. The conventional world is interested almost exclusively in becoming: i.e. How things or people might be changed or how they are simply changing over time as a natural process. It is usual to consider that the natural changes over time are hardly ultimately desirable, and vast industries have arisen to help us ward off the change or what we become over time. So it seems many of us have an interest, or desires, associated with changes over time, since there are positive, or desirable changes, and their opposite. The undesirable changes are in fact our motivators for much of what we do. The other great motivator is goal seeking, and goal seeking demands that we become eligible to be a contender in the reaching for some goal. So becoming, it would seem, is a range of activities, wherein we are either attempting to avoid something or get something or somewhere. We are so involved with the whole process of becoming, that this is all we feel that we are. We even describe ourselves in terms of our achievements or lack thereof and we have emotional responses to our successes and so-called failures and many programming devices designed to lift us out of the doom and gloom of the becoming process, such as: ‘There is no use crying over spilt milk’ or ‘Today is the first day of the rest of my life’ or ‘Always look on the bright side of life’ or even the morbidly funny: ‘I’d have to brighten up to kill myself.’
What is wrong with being focused on only what is happening with the passage of time is principally that we have grounded our being exclusively in what is only changeable and as a concommital with this, we believe, with much prompting, that we can actually ‘enact’ some of these changes, i.e. we are the ‘doers’. Yet, is it ‘actually true’ that we are capable of ‘doing’ anything, i.e. that there is actually an actor who does ‘things’ like become this or that, have ‘bad luck’ or ‘good luck’, become successful and so on? Because, if there is no such ‘one’, then what is coming to pass is simply coming to pass, without any ‘real’ involvement on our behalf. So can it be shown that there is no ‘actor’ or ‘doer’? If we observe our relationship to this vision of the world, we only know it through what the body shows to us through our nerve impulses, i.e. the brain is designed to create a picture out of a host of nerve responses to a supposed ‘actual world’. Therefore, we only know the world indirectly and since this is so, we are only in the position of simply viewing this picture. Further, we see that there is no actual ‘centre’ to this body-brain picture, it is simply ‘occurring’ and so we are only observers and as such we can never become anything as an act of something called the ‘will’.
What we see in being simply an ‘observer’, is that we are not truly implicated by what is simply ‘happening’ and the ‘show’ we seem to be implicated in, we have somehow chosen from some cosmic ‘amusement’ page (even though we have not necessarily found the ‘show’ to be ‘amusing’ and would perhaps like a refund.) So there is no becoming that has ‘us’ at its centre. We simply are, we only have ‘being’ and are therefore not that which can ‘gain’ or ‘lose’ from any of this ‘becoming’. Therefore what is required of us as ‘observers’ is to simply allow ourselves to be ‘literally’ and ‘only’ established in the disposition of the ‘observer’, totally devoid of any twisted motivations, until this awakened disposition has conclusively dissolved any tendency toward wanting to become anything in particular or even everything in general. In this way, finally, there is nothing arising that can modify our behaviour and we are free at last from all the ‘bondage’ of the ‘dilemma’ of ‘becoming’. There is only ‘being’ and all ‘else’, “only seems so” and at last, the ‘else’ passes out of sight and we discover we could never have been ‘something else.’
