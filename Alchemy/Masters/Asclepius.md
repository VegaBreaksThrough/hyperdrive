Asclepieion the temple of healing (healing baths)
Bethesda (pool of Bethesda)
Beth hesda (בית חסד/חסדא), means either "house of mercy" or "house of grace". This meaning may have been thought appropriate, since the location was seen as a place of disgrace due to the presence of invalids, and as a place of grace due to the granting of healing.

Asclepius is a figure from ancient Greek mythology, revered as the god of medicine and healing. His mythological background, symbolism, and influence on medicine are key to understanding his significance. Here are the fundamentals of Asclepius:

### **1. Mythological Background**
- **Parentage:** Asclepius is traditionally considered the son of Apollo, the god of prophecy, music, and healing, and a mortal woman named Coronis. According to myth, Coronis was unfaithful to Apollo, leading to her death. However, Apollo saved the unborn Asclepius from her womb and entrusted him to the centaur Chiron, who taught him the art of medicine.
- **Divine [[Powers]]:** Asclepius was said to possess remarkable healing abilities, surpassing even those of his father, Apollo. His skills became so advanced that he could revive the dead, which ultimately led to his downfall.

### **2. Symbolism and Attributes**
- **Rod of Asclepius:** The most recognized symbol associated with Asclepius is the Rod of Asclepius, a staff with a single serpent entwined around it. This symbol is widely used in medical contexts and represents healing and medicine.
- **Serpents:** Serpents were seen as sacred animals in the cult of Asclepius, symbolizing renewal and the dual nature of medicine, which can heal or harm.

### **3. Temples and Cult Worship**
- **Asclepieia:** Temples dedicated to Asclepius, known as Asclepieia, were established throughout ancient Greece and the Roman Empire. These temples served as healing centers where the sick would go to seek cures. Patients often practiced "incubation," a ritual where they slept in the temple, hoping to receive healing dreams or visions from the god.
- **Rituals:** The cult of Asclepius involved various healing rituals, offerings, and sacrifices. The god was believed to offer guidance and treatment through dreams, and priests, known as Asclepiades, would interpret these dreams to provide remedies.

### **4. Family and Legacy**
- **Hygeia and Panacea:** Asclepius had several children who personified different aspects of health and medicine. His daughters include Hygeia (goddess of health and cleanliness), Panacea (goddess of universal remedy), Iaso (goddess of recuperation), and Aegle (goddess of radiant good health). His sons, Machaon and Podalirius, were also associated with healing.
- **Conflict with Zeus:** Asclepius’s ability to resurrect the dead angered Zeus, who feared that this power could upset the natural order. As a result, Zeus struck Asclepius down with a thunderbolt, but due to Apollo’s plea, he was later placed among the stars as the constellation Ophiuchus, the serpent-bearer.

### **5. Influence on Medicine**
- **Historical Impact:** Asclepius's myth influenced the development of medicine in ancient Greece and Rome. The Hippocratic Oath, an ethical code for physicians, is sometimes associated with Asclepius and his teachings.
- **Legacy:** Asclepius is often considered a foundational figure in the history of medicine. His influence extended into the Renaissance and continues to be recognized in modern medical symbolism and practice.

### **6. Comparison with Other Figures**
- **Contrast with Apollo:** While Apollo represents broader aspects of prophecy, the arts, and healing, Asclepius is more specifically focused on practical medicine and the process of healing. 
- **Syncretism:** Asclepius was sometimes identified with other healing gods in different cultures, such as the Egyptian god Imhotep, demonstrating his widespread influence and the blending of cultural practices.

Asclepius’s legacy as a healer and a symbol of medicine endures, making him a central figure in both mythological and historical contexts related to health and healing.
