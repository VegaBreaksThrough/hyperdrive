Carl Jung, a Swiss psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, is best known for his contributions to analytical psychology. His theories have had a profound impact on psychology, psychotherapy, and even art and literature. 

Three main parts to Carl Jung's work:

# Consciousness
Field of awareness. Where we form our ego.
## [[Realisation|Individuation Process]]
- **Definition**: Individuation is the process of integrating different aspects of the psyche to achieve self-[[Realisation|realization]] and wholeness. It involves reconciling various parts of the personality, including the conscious and unconscious elements.
- **Goal**: The ultimate goal of individuation is to become the person one is inherently meant to be, achieving psychological [[A0309|balance]] and harmony.
# Personal Unconscious
Unique to us. Mainly the Shadow, the Animus/Anima, the Persona and the Self
**The Key Difference**

• The **Shadow** projection is usually what we reject and judge in others.

• The **Anima/Animus** projection is usually what we long for or idealize in others.
# [[The Unconscious|Collective Unconscious]]**
- **Definition**: Jung proposed that beneath the personal unconscious lies a deeper layer, the collective unconscious, which contains universal experiences and archetypes shared across humanity.
- **Archetypes**: These are innate, universal symbols and themes that shape human experiences, such as the Hero, the Mother, the Shadow, and the Anima/Animus.
Deepest part, inherited. This is where archetypes reside. Lots of regliious and mythological symbology.

## [[Archetypes]]
- **Definition**: Archetypes are fundamental, universal symbols and patterns that arise from the collective unconscious. They manifest in dreams, myths, and cultural narratives.
- **Examples**: Common archetypes include the Wise Old Man, the Great Mother, the Hero, and the Shadow.

### [[A0406|The Persona]]
- **Function**: The social mask or facade individuals present to the world. It helps individuals adapt to social roles and norms, but may mask their true selves.
### [[The Self]]
- **Definition**: In Jungian psychology, the Self is the central archetype representing the whole person, integrating both conscious and unconscious aspects. It symbolizes the unity and totality of the psyche.
- **Symbolism**: The Self is often represented in dreams and myths as a circle, mandala, or other symbols of wholeness.

### [[The Shadow]]
- **Definition**: The Shadow represents the parts of the self that are repressed, denied, or disowned. It embodies the darker, unconscious aspects of the personality.
- **Integration**: Jung believed that acknowledging and integrating the Shadow is essential for personal growth and self-awareness.

### [[The Animus-Anima]]
- **Definition**: The Anima represents the feminine aspects within the male psyche, while the Animus represents the masculine aspects within the female psyche.
- **Purpose**: These concepts are crucial for understanding gender dynamics within the psyche and achieving psychological [[A0309|balance]].


# Other Key Concepts
### Synchronicity
- **Definition**: Jung coined the term synchronicity to describe meaningful coincidences that are not causally related but have significant personal meaning.
- **Concept**: It highlights the connection between inner psychological states and external events.

### Psychological Types
- **Definition**: Jung developed a theory of psychological types, categorizing individuals into types based on their dominant functions and attitudes. The primary functions are Thinking, Feeling, Sensation, and Intuition.
- **Attitudes**: The two main attitudes are Introversion and Extraversion, reflecting how individuals relate to the world.

### Dream Analysis
- **Definition**: Jung viewed dreams as a way to access the unconscious and understand personal and collective symbols. He believed that dreams provide insight into the hidden aspects of the psyche.
- **Approach**: Unlike Freud, who emphasized sexual content in dreams, Jung focused on the symbolic and archetypal meanings.

### Myth and Religion
- **Definition**: Jung saw myths and religious symbols as expressions of universal archetypes and the collective unconscious. He believed that they play a vital role in understanding human experience and psychological development.

Jung's ideas continue to influence contemporary psychology, psychotherapy, and various fields of human inquiry. His work provides a rich framework for exploring the depths of the human psyche and the universal patterns that shape our experiences.
