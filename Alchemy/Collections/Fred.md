Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) was an Austrian neurologist and the founder of psychoanalysis. His theories have had a profound impact on psychology, psychiatry, and the understanding of human behavior. Here are the fundamentals of Freud's work:

### **1. Psychoanalysis**
- **Core Idea:** Psychoanalysis is a method for exploring unconscious processes and resolving psychological conflicts. It involves techniques such as free association, dream analysis, and transference to uncover hidden motivations and repressed [[Memory|memories]].
- **Therapeutic Goals:** To bring unconscious thoughts and feelings to the conscious mind, facilitating personal insight and emotional healing.

### **2. Structure of the Psyche**
- **Id:** Represents the primal, unconscious desires and instincts. It operates on the pleasure principle, seeking immediate gratification of needs and desires.
- **Ego:** Functions as the rational, decision-making part of the psyche. It operates on the reality principle, negotiating between the demands of the id, the superego, and the external world.
- **Superego:** Represents internalized moral standards and societal norms. It functions as the conscience, guiding the ego by imposing ethical standards and ideals.

### **3. Psychosexual Development**
- **Stages:** Freud proposed that personality develops through a series of psychosexual stages:
  - **Oral Stage (0-1 year):** Focus on oral activities like sucking and biting.
  - **Anal Stage (1-3 years):** Focus on control and autonomy related to bowel and bladder function.
  - **Phallic Stage (3-6 years):** Focus on the genital area and the Oedipus/Electra complex.
  - **Latency Stage (6-12 years):** Period of relative calm and focus on social and intellectual development.
  - **Genital Stage (12 years and up):** Focus on mature sexual relationships and integration of previous stages.

### **4. Defense Mechanisms**
- **Concept:** Defense mechanisms are unconscious strategies used by the ego to protect itself from anxiety and distress. Common mechanisms include:
  - **Repression:** Blocking distressing thoughts from consciousness.
  - **Denial:** Refusing to accept reality or facts.
  - **Projection:** Attributing one's own unacceptable feelings or thoughts to others.
  - **Displacement:** Redirecting emotions from a threatening target to a safer one.
  - **Sublimation:** Channeling unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities.

### **5. Dream Analysis**
- **Theory:** Freud believed that dreams are a window into the unconscious mind and that they reveal repressed desires and conflicts.
- **Techniques:** He used dream analysis to uncover the latent content (hidden meaning) behind the manifest content (actual storyline) of dreams.

### **6. Oedipus Complex**
- **Concept:** The Oedipus complex describes a child's feelings of desire for the opposite-sex parent and jealousy and rivalry with the same-sex parent. Freud viewed this as a critical stage in psychosexual development, particularly in boys.
- **Electra Complex:** The female counterpart to the Oedipus complex, involving similar dynamics but with the girl desiring the father and competing with the mother.

### **7. Sexuality and Repression**
- **Theory:** Freud argued that much of human behavior is influenced by sexual drives and that societal norms often repress these drives. He believed that repressed sexual desires could lead to psychological disorders.

### **8. Free Association**
- **Technique:** A method where patients are encouraged to verbalize any thoughts that come to mind without censorship. This technique helps to uncover unconscious material and unresolved conflicts.

### **9. Impact and Legacy**
- **Influence:** Freud’s ideas laid the foundation for modern psychotherapy and significantly influenced literature, art, and cultural theory. Despite controversies and critiques, his work remains a key part of psychological and philosophical discourse.

Freud’s theories, while groundbreaking, have been subject to extensive critique and revision. Many aspects of psychoanalysis have been challenged or evolved, but Freud’s foundational concepts continue to shape the field of psychology.
