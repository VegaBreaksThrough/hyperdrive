# The Will Of God
In the latter part of the 19th century the Katzenjammer Kids was a comic strip about a family situation where the children were wont to mischievously threaten destruction of home and parents with explosives. When they were caught at these little pranks they were severely punished with wooden paddles and they howled mightily.
In modern times the position has worsened, if the Neil Young song, Crime in the City, is any indication. He says in this song, speaking about a ‘cop’: “...his best friend was a robber and his wife was a thief, all the children were killers, they couldn’t get no relief...” The situation described here may be an extreme situation for most of us, yet it does point to the widespread decay, even demise, of the family as a serving social presence. Some families, perhaps most, manage to present a reasonable social face, yet often we do not have to look far for signs of the underlying pain. Often this is seen in behavioural disorders of the children within the social milieu of the school or simply the all but complete identification with the peer group that sees the parental class as seriously deranged aliens and vice versa.
The conventional structure of the 20th century family has hardly ever been seen as a nurturing presence and has most often merely bound all members in an obnoxious unenlightened clutch, serving to perpetuate all the ‘evils’ on into the future. At this time families tend to represent, in their ritual associations with each other, the greatest obstruction to their mutual growth into the human relationship. If [[Truth]] is not apparent at the family level it cannot suddenly appear within the wider community.
The fire of this alchymeic [[Crucible]] invests us with the capacity to create new adaptations in relation to each other every day. To create new adaptations has its importance in dissolving the fearful conventional rituals we tend to enact in unconsciousness. Therefore in the awakening that is new adaptations, rather than families re-enforcing each other’s tendencies, they can begin to serve their mutual release from the fruitless destiny that arises in the dramatisation of mere tendency. “Dot is luffley.”
